## Introduction

The flood ends the world of Cain, but Genesis does not present it as the end of the problem that world had displayed. Eight people leave the ark, and within a few chapters the narrator describes a new kingdom, a new city and a new act of collective pride. This study follows that development. It begins with the family of Seth, through which the line of promise reaches Noah, then examines the post-flood world described in the table of nations, the figure of Nimrod, the question whether fallen angels are thought to have influenced him, and the narrative of Babel. The Bible does not say that a fallen angel fathered Nimrod. Genesis calls him a son of Cush and a grandson of Ham, and the discussion below distinguishes consistently between what the text says, what later traditions add, and what historians infer.

## Seth and the line of promise

[Genesis 4:25](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A25&version=ESV) records the birth of Seth in terms that look back to Abel: "God has appointed for me another offspring instead of Abel, for Cain killed him." The name is connected with the verb "to appoint," and [Genesis 5:3](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+5%3A3&version=ESV) says that Adam "fathered a son in his own likeness, after his image, and named him Seth," language that deliberately recalls the creation of humanity in God's image ([Genesis 1:26-27](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+1%3A26-27&version=ESV); 5:1). The image of God is therefore presented as surviving the fall and passing from one generation to the next.

The genealogy of [Genesis 5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+5&version=ESV) is both a record of promise and a record of mortality. The phrase "and he died" occurs eight times, which the narrator uses to illustrate the consequences of Adam's disobedience (compare [Romans 5:12-14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans+5%3A12-14&version=ESV)), and the line nevertheless leads to Noah. The New Testament traces the ancestry of Jesus through Seth ([Luke 3:23-38](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+3%3A23-38&version=ESV)). [Genesis 4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4&version=ESV) and 5 also invite comparison with each other. Both genealogies contain a Lamech and an Enoch, and several other names are similar (Irad and Jared, Mehujael and Mahalalel, Methushael and Methuselah), which most commentators take as evidence of deliberate parallelism (Wenham 1987; Westermann 1984). Cain's Lamech composes a song of vengeance ([Genesis 4:23-24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A23-24&version=ESV)), while Seth's Lamech names his son in hope: "this one shall bring us relief from our work" ([Genesis 5:29](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+5%3A29&version=ESV)). Between them stands Enoch, who "walked with God, and he was not, for God took him" ([Genesis 5:24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+5%3A24&version=ESV); compare [Jude 14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jude+1%3A14&version=ESV)).

Ancient interpreters did not assume that Seth's line remained faithful. [Genesis 4:26](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A26&version=ESV) says that "people began to call upon the name of the LORD" in the days of Enosh, and Jewish tradition read the verse in two opposite ways. Some took it as the beginning of public worship, while the Aramaic Targums and later Maimonides (*Mishneh Torah*, Laws of Idolatry 1:1) understood it as the time when idolatry began; Maimonides writes that "in the days of Enosh" mankind erred and began to honour the stars as God's servants. Josephus (*Antiquities* 1.2.3) adds that Seth's descendants were virtuous for seven generations before they changed, and that they studied the heavenly bodies and recorded their discoveries on two pillars. These details are not in Genesis. Later Gnostic groups of the second to fourth centuries claimed Seth as a spiritual ancestor, and historians call them Sethians, but their literature has no historical connection with the Seth of Genesis.

## Noah and the new beginning

After the flood God repeats the creation command, "Be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth" ([Genesis 9:1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+9%3A1&version=ESV)), and this command is important for the Babel narrative. [Genesis 10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+10&version=ESV) then lists the descendants of Shem, Ham and Japheth. The list is organised by "clans, languages, lands and nations" ([Genesis 10:5, 20, 31](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+10%3A5%2C+20%2C+31&version=ESV)), and since it already speaks of separate languages it must be read as an anticipation of the dispersion described in [Genesis 11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+11&version=ESV) and not as its sequel. Jewish tradition counted seventy nations in the table (fourteen descended from Japheth, thirty from Ham and twenty-six from Shem), and the number becomes significant in later discussions of the nations and their heavenly patrons.

The post-flood period also raises the question whether the evils of the earlier world survived. [Genesis 6:4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+6%3A4&version=ESV) says that the Nephilim were on the earth "in those days, and also afterward," [Numbers 13:33](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Numbers+13%3A33&version=ESV) reports that Israel's spies saw Nephilim in Canaan, and Deuteronomy mentions peoples called Rephaim and Anakim, including King Og, whose bed was "of iron" ([Deuteronomy 3:11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy+3%3A11&version=ESV)). Interpreters disagree whether these texts describe actual giants or the exaggeration of frightened observers. Second Temple Jewish writers addressed the problem more directly. According to 1 Enoch 15, the spirits that came from the slain giants remain on the earth as evil spirits, and *Jubilees* 10 says that after the flood demons began to lead Noah's grandchildren astray; Noah prays for protection, and the accuser (*Mastema*) obtains permission to keep one tenth of the spirits under his authority. These books are not part of the biblical canon, but they show how some ancient Jews explained the rise of idolatry and tyranny after the flood (Reed 2005; Stuckenbruck 2014).

## Nimrod

Nimrod is introduced within the table of nations:

> "Cush fathered Nimrod; he was the first on earth to be a mighty man. He was a mighty hunter before the LORD." — [Genesis 10:8-9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+10%3A8-9&version=ESV) (ESV)

The text continues that "the beginning of his kingdom was Babel, Erech, and Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar," and that "from that land he went into Assyria and built Nineveh" and other cities ([Genesis 10:10-12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+10%3A10-12&version=ESV)). He appears again in [1 Chronicles 1:10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Chronicles+1%3A10&version=ESV), and [Micah 5:6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Micah+5%3A6&version=ESV) uses "the land of Nimrod" as a name for Assyria. Several details are significant. The Hebrew clause that the ESV renders "he was the first on earth to be a mighty man" can also be translated "he began to be a mighty man on the earth" (see the ESV footnote and the KJV), so "first" is an interpretation and not a certainty. The Hebrew word translated "mighty man" (*gibbôr*) is related to the "mighty men" (*gibbôrîm*) of [Genesis 6:4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+6%3A4&version=ESV), and the Septuagint renders it in both places by the Greek word for "giant," a link that Augustine noticed (*City of God* 16.3). The phrase "before the LORD" can mean "in the sight of," "in the presence of" or "against" the LORD, and the choice of translation usually expresses the translator's judgment of Nimrod. Babel, Erech (Uruk) and Accad (Akkad) were major cities of ancient Mesopotamia, and "Shinar" corresponds to Sumer.

Ancient interpreters tended to judge Nimrod severely. The *Targum Pseudo-Jonathan* on [Genesis 10:9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+10%3A9&version=ESV) calls him mighty in hunting and in wickedness before the LORD, and a rebel. Josephus (*Antiquities* 1.4.2) says that he persuaded the people to attribute their prosperity to their own courage and not to God, gradually turned the government into a tyranny, and wished to build a tower that another flood could not reach. Later rabbinic tradition (for example *Eruvin* 53a) connects the name with the Hebrew root for rebellion (*mārad*), and still later legends, which have no support in the Bible, make him Abraham's persecutor. Augustine (*City of God* 16.3-4) read the Greek *enantion* as "against" and not "before," described Nimrod as a hunter in the sense of "deceiver, oppressor, and destroyer," and linked him with the building of the tower, which he interprets as an expression of impious pride.

Historians have not identified Nimrod with certainty. Proposals include Sargon of Akkad, Naram-Sin, the Assyrian king Tukulti-Ninurta I (whom Speiser 1964 favoured), the early Sumerian ruler Enmerkar of Uruk, and a connection between the name and the hunter-god Ninurta. The biblical description fits a Mesopotamian king who founded cities and a kingdom, but the text is too short to support a firm identification. One popular theory should be set aside. Alexander Hislop's *The Two Babylons* (1853) claimed that Nimrod, his supposed wife Semiramis and a son Tammuz stood at the origin of pagan religion. Historians reject this reconstruction: the historical Semiramis (Shammuramat) was an Assyrian queen of the late ninth century BC, centuries after any date proposed for Nimrod, and no ancient text links the three figures as Hislop does.

## Did fallen angels cause Nimrod?

The question is a natural one given the connection of Nimrod with the *gibbôrîm* of [Genesis 6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+6&version=ESV), and it must be answered at three levels. At the level of the biblical text, the answer is no: Nimrod is a human being, the son of Cush, and Genesis attributes his kingdom to human ambition. At the level of the wider biblical theology, the New Testament holds that human conduct can be influenced by spiritual powers. The world "lies in the power of the evil one" ([1 John 5:19](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+John+5%3A19&version=ESV)), Satan is "the god of this world" who blinds unbelievers ([2 Corinthians 4:4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2+Corinthians+4%3A4&version=ESV)), and believers struggle against "the rulers, against the authorities, against the cosmic powers over this present darkness" ([Ephesians 6:12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ephesians+6%3A12&version=ESV); see Arnold 1992). These texts do not suppress human responsibility, and they say nothing about Nimrod specifically. At the level of later tradition, 1 Enoch and *Jubilees* trace continuing evil to the spirits of the fallen Watchers and their offspring, and Justin Martyr (*Second Apology* 5) taught that demons descended from the fallen angels enslaved humanity. These are interpretations valuable to historians but not Scripture.

A cautious conclusion is that the idea of spiritual influence on Nimrod and the builders of Babel is a legitimate theological inference from the wider canon, but that Genesis itself locates the cause in human pride.

## Babel

The narrative of Babel ([Genesis 11:1-9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+11%3A1-9&version=ESV)) is structured around a series of wordplays and reversals. The people migrate to the plain of Shinar, make baked bricks and use bitumen for mortar, the standard building materials of Mesopotamia, and say:

> "Come, let us build ourselves a city and a tower with its top in the heavens, and let us make a name for ourselves, lest we be dispersed over the face of the whole earth." — [Genesis 11:4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+11%3A4&version=ESV) (ESV)

Each clause names a motive. The builders gather to avoid scattering, which contradicts the command of [Genesis 9:1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+9%3A1&version=ESV). They seek to "make a name" for themselves instead of relying on God's. And they build a structure they hope will secure them. God's response is ironic: "the LORD came down to see the city and the tower" ([Genesis 11:5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+11%3A5&version=ESV)), because what the builders thought reached the heavens required a descent to be seen. He confuses their language, and the name Babel is explained by the Hebrew verb *bālal*, "to confuse" ([Genesis 11:9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+11%3A9&version=ESV)), a play on the Akkadian *bāb-ilim*, "gate of the god(s)."

The historical background is well documented. The ziggurat, a stepped temple tower, was a characteristic form of Mesopotamian religious architecture. The best known example in Babylon was Etemenanki, "the house of the foundation of heaven and earth," rebuilt by Nebuchadnezzar II (c. 604-562 BC) and depicted on the so-called Tower of Babel Stele, and Herodotus (*Histories* 1.181-183) described the temple tower of Babylon and called it the temple of Zeus Belus. The Babylonian creation epic *Enuma Elish* says that the gods built Babylon, and Genesis can be read as a reply in which human beings build the city and God judges it (Walton 2006). A Sumerian text, *Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta*, includes an incantation referring to a time when all peoples spoke one language, which S. N. Kramer (1968) compared with [Genesis 11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+11&version=ESV), although the interpretation of the passage is disputed. Most scholars regard the biblical story as shaped by the Babylonian ziggurat tradition, and some have proposed older towers such as the one at Eridu, but Genesis does not identify the tower or give a date.

Jewish interpreters also saw a spiritual dimension in the scattering. *Jubilees* 10:22-26 says that God descended with angels and that a wind overthrew the tower, and the *Targum Pseudo-Jonathan* on [Genesis 11:7-8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+11%3A7-8&version=ESV) has God addressing the seventy angels who stand before him. That legend of seventy angels, attached to the seventy nations, appears in a developed form only in later texts such as the Targum and *Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer* 24, which are usually dated to the early medieval period. The underlying idea, that the nations are assigned to heavenly beings, is much older, as Sirach 17:17, *Jubilees* 15:31-32 and the Qumran text of [Deuteronomy 32:8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy+32%3A8&version=ESV) show, and the next study examines that evidence.

## Babylon in the rest of the Bible

Babylon becomes a standing symbol of organised human pride. Isaiah's taunt against the king of Babylon, "I will ascend to heaven... I will make myself like the Most High" ([Isaiah 14:13-14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+14%3A13-14&version=ESV)), echoes the tower "with its top in the heavens," and Christian interpreters have often read the passage as pointing beyond the king to the devil. Nebuchadnezzar's boast, "Is not this great Babylon, which I have built?" ([Daniel 4:30](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Daniel+4%3A30&version=ESV)), is followed by his humbling, and [Revelation 17-18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Revelation+17-18&version=ESV) describes "Babylon the great" as the symbol of the world's rebellious order, a city that has become "a dwelling place for demons" ([Revelation 18:2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Revelation+18%3A2&version=ESV)). The New Testament also supplies a counterpart to Babel: at Pentecost people from "every nation under heaven" hear the apostles in their own languages ([Acts 2:5-11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts+2%3A5-11&version=ESV)), and the church is finally gathered "from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages" ([Revelation 7:9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Revelation+7%3A9&version=ESV)).

## Conclusion

The line of Seth carried the image of God and the hope of a deliverer through the flood, and Noah's family inherited the command to fill the earth. Within a few generations Nimrod, the first "mighty man" of the new world, and the builders of Babel had repeated the pattern of pride that Genesis traces from Cain. The text does not say that fallen angels fathered Nimrod, but the New Testament's teaching about spiritual influence allows the question to be asked, and later Jewish and Christian writers asked it. The dispersion at Babel leaves a further question open, which concerns what the scattered nations worshiped and where their gods came from, and that is the subject of the final study.
