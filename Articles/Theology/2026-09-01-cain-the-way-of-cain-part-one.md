## Introduction

The fourth chapter of Genesis contains the first murder in the biblical narrative, and the chapters that follow trace its consequences until the earth is described as "filled with violence" and the flood is announced. This study begins with Cain. It examines how Genesis tells his story, where biblical and Jewish tradition locates the source of his evil, and how the narrator uses the family of Cain to introduce the problem that the flood narrative will address. It is the first of four studies that follow the same narrative thread through the flood, the tower of Babel, and the question of the gods of the nations.

Two limits should be stated at the outset. First, the Bible nowhere says that Cain was an angel, a demon or a giant. Those ideas belong to later retellings, and this study explains where they arise. Second, where Jewish interpreters, Christian interpreters and historians have read a passage differently, each reading is presented with its date and its evidence, and the study distinguishes consistently among what the biblical text states, what later tradition adds, and what historians infer.

## The narrative of [Genesis 4:1-16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A1-16&version=ESV)

The story opens with the mother's words at the birth of her first son: "I have gotten a man with the help of the LORD" ([Genesis 4:1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A1&version=ESV)). The name *Qayin* is explained by the verb *qānâ*, "to acquire," a wordplay typical of Genesis. The name also has a cognate meaning "smith" in other Semitic languages, which has led some scholars to link it with the Kenites, the metalworking clan mentioned elsewhere in the Old Testament (see [Genesis 4:22](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A22&version=ESV) for Tubal-cain), although the narrator himself offers only the explanation of acquisition (Wenham 1987; Westermann 1984). Eve's words are often read in light of the promise of [Genesis 3:15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+3%3A15&version=ESV), and the sentence suggests that she hoped her son would be the promised offspring who would defeat the serpent. The story that follows shows how that hope was disappointed.

The brothers bring offerings, and the narrator reports only that the LORD "had regard" for Abel and his offering but not for Cain and his ([Genesis 4:3-5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A3-5&version=ESV)). The text does not state the reason. Interpreters have proposed several. Some point to the nature of the gifts, since Abel brought the firstborn of his flock while Cain brought "some of the fruit of the ground." Others emphasise the contrast in attitude. The New Testament takes the second route: "By faith Abel offered to God a more acceptable sacrifice than Cain, through which he was commended as righteous" ([Hebrews 11:4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews+11%3A4&version=ESV)). The first-century historian Josephus (*Antiquities* 1.2.1) describes Cain as greedy and wholly intent on gain, and Genesis Rabbah 22:7 imagines a quarrel between the brothers over the division of the world. These sources, from different religious communities and centuries, agree that the story concerns the condition of Cain's heart more than the physical content of the offering.

## The warning and the choice

The theological centre of the narrative is God's address to Cain before the murder:

> "If you do well, will you not be accepted? And if you do not do well, sin is crouching at the door. Its desire is contrary to yours, but you must rule over it." — [Genesis 4:7](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A7&version=ESV) (ESV)

The Hebrew of this verse is among the most difficult in Genesis. The participle translated "crouching" (*rōbēṣ*) has been compared with the Akkadian *rābiṣu*, a lurking demon at the threshold, a suggestion made by E. A. Speiser in his Anchor Bible commentary and discussed by Wenham (1987). The phrase "its desire is contrary to yours, but you must rule over it" repeats the wording of [Genesis 3:16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+3%3A16&version=ESV), which links the two narratives. Whatever the details of translation, the verse makes three claims: that Cain has a way back ("will you not be accepted?"), that sin is portrayed as an external force that threatens him, and that he is nonetheless able to master it.

Cain's response is to take his brother into the field and kill him ([Genesis 4:8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A8&version=ESV)). When asked where Abel is, he lies: "I do not know; am I my brother's keeper?" ([Genesis 4:9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A9&version=ESV)). God's reply, that Abel's blood "is crying to me from the ground" ([Genesis 4:10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A10&version=ESV)), introduces a motif that runs through the rest of Genesis, the pollution of the land by innocent blood (compare [Genesis 9:6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+9%3A6&version=ESV)). Cain complains that his punishment is more than he can bear ([Genesis 4:13-14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A13-14&version=ESV)), but the narrator records no confession. The sequence of anger, warning, refusal, murder, denial and lack of repentance therefore presents Cain's evil as a series of choices and not as an inherited condition.

## The source of Cain's evil

The New Testament addresses the question of origin directly:

> "We should not be like Cain, who was of the evil one and murdered his brother. And why did he murder him? Because his own deeds were evil and his brother's righteous." — [1 John 3:12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+John+3%3A12&version=ESV) (ESV)

The author describes Cain as "of the evil one" (*ek tou ponērou*) but explains the statement by Cain's own deeds. The same letter says that the devil "has been sinning from the beginning" ([1 John 3:8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+John+3%3A8&version=ESV)), and in the Gospel of John Jesus calls the devil "a murderer from the beginning" ([John 8:44](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+8%3A44&version=ESV)). The most natural reading is moral and spiritual: Cain's act expresses the same hostility to God and to the righteous that the New Testament attributes to the devil, and Cain adopts it as his own. The text does not say that the devil was Cain's father in any physical sense. [Jude 11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jude+1%3A11&version=ESV) uses the phrase "the way of Cain" for envy, violence and self-will, and [Matthew 23:35](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+23%3A35&version=ESV) and [Hebrews 12:24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews+12%3A24&version=ESV) take Abel as the first of the righteous who suffer unjustly.

A different explanation appears in some later Jewish writings. The *Targum Pseudo-Jonathan*, an Aramaic paraphrase usually dated to the seventh century or later, renders [Genesis 4:1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A1&version=ESV) so that Eve conceives Cain from Sammael, a name for an angel of death, and *Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer* 21 contains a similar tradition. These are minority views in rabbinic literature and are late. The Hebrew text states that "Adam knew Eve his wife, and she conceived and bore Cain" ([Genesis 4:1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A1&version=ESV)), and nothing in the Hebrew text suggests that Cain had a father other than Adam. [Genesis 5:3](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+5%3A3&version=ESV) describes Seth as born in Adam's likeness, but it does not contrast him with Cain. Mainstream Christian interpretation has rejected the idea that Cain was fathered by Satan or by an angel. Some Gnostic groups of the second century did honour Cain and his line, as Irenaeus reports (*Against Heresies* 1.31), but that is a separate development that reverses the moral judgment of Genesis. The Sammael tradition is best understood as a later attempt to explain the origin of such a crime by giving the criminal a supernatural parentage.

The tradition does reflect a real biblical theme, the conflict announced in [Genesis 3:15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+3%3A15&version=ESV) between the offspring of the serpent and the offspring of the woman. In the biblical text that conflict is carried out through human choices and not through blood descent, and Cain's story is the first illustration of it.

## Cain's line ([Genesis 4:17-24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A17-24&version=ESV))

God's sentence on Cain combines judgment with protection. He is condemned to be "a fugitive and a wanderer" ([Genesis 4:12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A12&version=ESV)), and the LORD "put a mark on Cain, lest any who found him should attack him" ([Genesis 4:15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A15&version=ESV)). The Hebrew word (*ʾôt*) means a sign, and in context it functions as a protection and not as a punishment (Westermann 1984). Cain's departure "from the presence of the LORD" ([Genesis 4:16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A16&version=ESV)) then opens the genealogy of his descendants, in which the narrator records the first city ([Genesis 4:17](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A17&version=ESV)), nomadic herding, music, and the working of bronze and iron ([Genesis 4:20-22](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A20-22&version=ESV)).

Commentators disagree about the tone of this list. Westermann (1984) emphasises the ambivalence: the achievements of civilisation are presented without condemnation, but they develop in a family that has separated itself from God. The genealogy culminates in Lamech, who takes two wives and sings a boast of vengeance, the Song of Lamech: "If Cain's revenge is sevenfold, then Lamech's is seventy-sevenfold" ([Genesis 4:24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A24&version=ESV)). The progression from a single act of violence to a poem celebrating it in the seventh generation is a deliberate literary climax, and it explains the narrator's later verdict on the world before the flood.

## The counterpoint of Seth

The chapter closes with a second family. Seth is born, and "at that time people began to call upon the name of the LORD" ([Genesis 4:26](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A26&version=ESV)). The genealogy of [Genesis 5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+5&version=ESV) then traces Seth's line to Noah and includes Enoch, who "walked with God" ([Genesis 5:24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+5%3A24&version=ESV)). The two genealogies share several names (Enoch, Lamech, and similar forms such as Irad and Jared, Mehujael and Mahalalel), and scholars generally take this as evidence that the narrator has arranged parallel lines for comparison (Wenham 1987). The effect is to set side by side two kinds of human community, one that develops without reference to God and one that is defined by its relation to him. What happens when these two communities meet is the subject of [Genesis 6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+6&version=ESV), and it is the question of the next study.

## Conclusion

Genesis presents Cain as a person who was warned, was offered a way of repentance, and chose violence. Both the New Testament and Jewish interpreters locate the cause of his evil in his own deeds, though the New Testament also sees in it the influence of the devil, while some later rabbinic traditions attribute it to supernatural parentage. The narrator then follows the consequences through Cain's descendants, so that the story of one murder becomes the story of a culture in which violence is celebrated. The next question, raised by the juxtaposition of the two families in [Genesis 4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4&version=ESV) and 5, concerns the meeting of those lines in [Genesis 6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+6&version=ESV).
