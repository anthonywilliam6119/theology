Mary as the Mother of God: Biblical, Historical, and Theological Foundations

By Anthony William · Published September 26, 2026

Few Christian teachings concerning the Virgin Mary have generated as much theological discussion as the Catholic confession that Mary is truly the Mother of God, traditionally expressed by the Greek title Theotokos (Θεοτόκος).

At first, this expression may sound confusing. How can a human woman be called "Mother of God"? Did Mary exist before God? Did Mary create God's divine nature? Does the Catholic Church believe that Mary is divine?

The Catholic answer to these questions is no. The title does not mean that Mary is the origin of God's divinity. Rather, it means that the child whom Mary conceived and gave birth to is the eternal Son of God who became truly human.

The Catechism of the Catholic Church explicitly explains that the person Mary conceived according to humanity was none other than the eternal Son of the Father.

Therefore, the question of Mary ultimately leads to an even greater question: Who is Jesus Christ?

Mary in the New Testament

The New Testament presents Mary most extensively in the opening chapters of Matthew and Luke, but her presence continues throughout the Gospel story and into the early Church.

Matthew describes the conception of Jesus:

"Before they came together she was found to be with child of the Holy Spirit." — Matthew 1:18

Luke records the angel Gabriel's announcement to Mary:

"The Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you; therefore the child to be born will be called holy, the Son of God." — Luke 1:35

This is important because Luke identifies Mary's child as the Son of God.

John's Gospel goes even further:

"In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." — John 1:1

John then declares:

"And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us." — John 1:14

The Christian claim is therefore that the eternal Word of God became flesh. The one who became flesh was born from Mary.

"The Mother of My Lord" — Luke 1:43

One of the most important biblical passages in the discussion of Mary's divine motherhood occurs during Mary's visit to Elizabeth.

"And why is this granted me, that the mother of my Lord should come to me?" — Luke 1:43

Luke identifies Elizabeth as being filled with the Holy Spirit:

"Elizabeth was filled with the Holy Spirit." — Luke 1:41

Elizabeth therefore calls Mary "the mother of my Lord" while Mary is still carrying Jesus in her womb.

The Catholic Catechism specifically connects Luke 1:43 with the doctrine of the Mother of God. Read CCC 495 — Mary's Divine Motherhood

What Does "Mother of God" Actually Mean?

The title Mother of God does not mean that Mary existed before God or that she created God's divine nature.

Christianity teaches that Jesus Christ is truly God and truly man. The eternal Son possesses the divine nature eternally, while the human nature of Christ was truly received from Mary.

Therefore, Mary did not give Christ his divinity. She gave birth to the person who is the eternal Son of God according to his humanity.

The Catechism summarizes the Catholic position directly: Mary is truly Mother of God because she is the mother of the eternal Son of God made man.

The Meaning of Theotokos

The Greek word Theotokos is traditionally translated as "God-bearer" or "Mother of God."

The title became especially important because of a controversy concerning the identity of Jesus Christ.

The deeper question was: Who is the person whom Mary gave birth to?

If Jesus Christ is the eternal Word of God who became man, then Mary gave birth to the incarnate Word.

This is why Theotokos is fundamentally a Christological title.

The Council of Ephesus and Nestorius

During the fifth century, the meaning of Theotokos became the subject of a major controversy involving Nestorius, Patriarch of Constantinople, and Cyril of Alexandria.

The controversy reached the Council of Ephesus in A.D. 431.

The surviving documents of the Council include Cyril's correspondence with Nestorius and his Twelve Anathemas.

The controversy was fundamentally about Christology. Cyril defended the confession that the eternal Word truly became man and that Christ is one Person.

Theotokos or Christotokos? What Nestorius Actually Argued

It is a common misunderstanding to portray Nestorius as simply denying honor to Mary. His actual concern was more technical, and understanding it helps clarify what the Council of Ephesus was defending.

Nestorius preferred the title Christotokos — "Christ-bearer" — rather than Theotokos. His worry was that calling Mary "God-bearer" might imply that the divine nature itself originated in Mary's womb, as though her maternity applied directly to God's eternal being rather than to the human nature the Word assumed.

Nestorius's theology is often associated with a strong distinction between the divine and human natures in Christ — sometimes characterized by critics as dividing Christ into two separate subjects loosely joined together, though scholars continue to debate how fairly this represents his actual position, since much of his own writing survives only in fragments or through the reports of his opponents.

Cyril of Alexandria's response was that the person born of Mary is not a human person merely joined to the divine Word, but the divine Word himself, who took to himself a complete human nature from the moment of conception. On this reasoning, since the subject born of Mary is the eternal Son, Mary is rightly called Theotokos — not because she originates divinity, but because the one she bore is truly God.

The Council of Ephesus sided with Cyril's formulation. The Council of Chalcedon twenty years later (A.D. 451) refined the language further, affirming that Christ is one Person in two natures, "without confusion, without change, without division, without separation" — language intended to protect both Cyril's concern for the unity of Christ's person and legitimate concerns about preserving the integrity of each nature.

Why Theotokos Is Primarily About Jesus

The Word was God.
The Word became flesh.
Mary gave birth to Jesus.
Therefore, Mary gave birth to the incarnate Word.

The title Theotokos therefore protects the Christian confession concerning the identity of Jesus Christ.

Mary's Virginity

Matthew and Luke explicitly teach the virginal conception of Jesus.

"Before they came together she was found to be with child of the Holy Spirit." — Matthew 1:18
"How can this be, since I do not know man?" — Luke 1:34

Catholic Christianity additionally teaches Mary's perpetual virginity. Catechism — Mary's Virginity

What About the "Brothers" of Jesus?

One of the most frequently discussed objections to Mary's perpetual virginity concerns the New Testament references to the "brothers" of Jesus.

"Is not this the carpenter's son? Is not his mother called Mary? And are not his brothers James and Joseph and Simon and Judas?" — Matthew 13:55

The Greek word adelphoi normally means brothers, but questions of kinship terminology, literary context, and ancient family structures have generated significant scholarly discussion. Historically, three distinct positions have been proposed:

The Helvidian View

Named after the fourth-century writer Helvidius, this view holds that the "brothers" were biological children born to Mary and Joseph after Jesus. This is the interpretation most commonly held among Protestant traditions today, and it takes the term "brothers" in its most straightforward biological sense.

The Epiphanian View

Named after Epiphanius of Salamis, this view proposes that the "brothers" were Joseph's children from a previous marriage, making them Jesus's step-siblings rather than Mary's biological children. This view has some support in early apocryphal literature (such as the Protoevangelium of James) and remains common in Eastern Orthodox tradition.

The Hieronymian View

Named after Jerome, this view argues that "brothers" refers to cousins or more distant relatives, consistent with a broader semantic range for kinship terms in both Hebrew/Aramaic and the Greek used to translate them. This became the standard position in Western Catholic theology following Jerome's arguments in the fourth century.

Each position has been defended by serious scholars, and each carries different implications for Mary's perpetual virginity — the Epiphanian and Hieronymian views preserve it, while the Helvidian view does not.

Matthew 1:25 and the Word "Until"

Another frequently discussed passage is Matthew 1:25:

"He knew her not until she had given birth to a son."

Some interpreters argue that the word "until" implies that Joseph and Mary had marital relations after Jesus' birth.

Catholic interpretation responds that the word "until" does not necessarily state what happened after the point being described. The verse clearly establishes the virginal conception of Jesus, but it does not explicitly state that Mary later had other children.

The Immaculate Conception

The Immaculate Conception is often misunderstood. It does not mean that Jesus was conceived without sin. It refers to the conception of Mary herself.

Catholic doctrine teaches that Mary was preserved from original sin from the first moment of her conception by God's grace, through the merits of Christ.

Catechism — Immaculate Conception

Catholic theology frequently discusses Luke 1:28, where Gabriel addresses Mary with the Greek expression kecharitomene. However, Luke 1:28 by itself does not contain the complete dogmatic formulation of the Immaculate Conception. The Catholic argument is cumulative and involves Scripture, Tradition, theological reasoning, and the Church's later doctrinal definition, formally proclaimed by Pope Pius IX in 1854.

Mary as the New Eve

One of the oldest Christian theological comparisons is between Eve and Mary.

Eve hears the serpent and disobeys God's command. Mary hears Gabriel and responds:

"Behold, I am the handmaid of the Lord; let it be to me according to your word." — Luke 1:38

Early Christian writers such as Justin Martyr and Irenaeus developed the Eve–Mary parallel.

The Second Vatican Council's Lumen Gentium explicitly discusses this interpretation. Lumen Gentium — Mary and the New Eve

Mary and the Ark of the Covenant

Another Catholic biblical interpretation sees Mary as a typological fulfillment of the Old Testament Ark of the Covenant.

In 2 Samuel 6, David asks:

"How can the ark of the LORD come to me?" — 2 Samuel 6:9

In Luke 1:43, Elizabeth asks:

"And why is this granted me, that the mother of my Lord should come to me?"

Catholic interpreters also compare the three-month stay of the Ark with Mary's approximately three-month stay with Elizabeth, as well as David's joyful response before the Ark and John the Baptist's leaping in Elizabeth's womb.

This should be understood as biblical typology, not as a verse explicitly saying, "Mary is the Ark of the Covenant."

Mary at Cana

John 2 records Mary's role at the wedding at Cana.

"They have no wine." — John 2:3

Mary then tells the servants:

"Do whatever he tells you." — John 2:5

Jesus performs the miracle. Mary does not perform it herself.

Catholic theology therefore understands Cana as an important biblical example of Mary's intercessory role: she presents the need to Jesus, while Jesus remains the source of the miracle.

Lumen Gentium — Mary's Role in Salvation

Mary at the Cross

John records Mary standing near the Cross:

"When Jesus saw his mother, and the disciple whom he loved standing nearby, he said to his mother, 'Woman, behold, your son!'" — John 19:26
"Behold, your mother!" — John 19:27

Historically, the passage describes Jesus entrusting Mary to the beloved disciple. Catholic theology additionally sees a theological dimension in which Mary becomes associated with the spiritual motherhood of Christ's disciples.

Lumen Gentium — Mary as Mother in the Order of Grace

Mary and the Church

Mary is presented in Acts as praying together with the disciples:

"All these with one accord devoted themselves to prayer, together with the women and Mary the mother of Jesus." — Acts 1:14

Catholic theology understands Mary not as an alternative to the Church, but as a pre-eminent member and model of the Church.

Vatican II — Lumen Gentium, Chapter VIII

The Assumption of Mary

Catholic Christianity teaches that Mary was taken body and soul into heavenly glory. This doctrine is known as the Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary, formally defined as dogma by Pope Pius XII in 1950.

Unlike the virginal conception, the New Testament does not provide a direct narrative stating that Mary was assumed into heaven.

Catholic theology therefore understands the Assumption through Sacred Tradition, theological development, and the teaching authority of the Church.

Lumen Gentium — Mary's Assumption

Is the Woman in Revelation 12 Mary?

Revelation 12 describes a woman clothed with the sun who gives birth to a male child destined to rule the nations.

Because the child is associated with Christ, Christian interpreters have connected the woman with Mary. However, Revelation 12 contains rich symbolic imagery and has also been interpreted in relation to Israel, Zion, and the Church.

Therefore, it is more precise to say that Mary has an important typological relationship to the woman of Revelation 12 rather than claiming that the chapter simply states that the woman is Mary.

Is Mary a Goddess?

Catholic doctrine does not teach that Mary is a goddess.

Mary is a creature of God. She is completely dependent upon divine grace and, according to Catholic theology, is redeemed by Christ.

Catholic theology distinguishes the worship given to God from the special honor given to Mary — traditionally expressed through the Greek terms latria (worship due to God alone) and hyperdulia (the elevated veneration given specifically to Mary, distinct from the ordinary dulia given to other saints).

The Second Vatican Council states that Marian devotion differs essentially from the adoration offered to God.

Lumen Gentium — Marian Devotion and Worship

What About "One Mediator" in 1 Timothy 2:5?

A common Protestant objection to Marian intercession comes from 1 Timothy 2:5:

"For there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus." — 1 Timothy 2:5

Catholic theology fully affirms that Jesus Christ is the unique mediator of salvation.

However, Catholics distinguish between Christ's unique mediation and subordinate forms of intercession.

The same chapter asks Christians to make prayers and intercessions for others:

"I urge that supplications, prayers, intercessions, and thanksgivings be made for all men." — 1 Timothy 2:1

Catholic reasoning therefore asks whether asking Mary to pray is fundamentally different from asking another Christian to pray.

Lumen Gentium — Christ the One Mediator and Mary's Intercession

Mary in the Early Church Fathers

Marian theology did not suddenly appear during the Middle Ages. Early Christian writers already discussed Mary's virginity, obedience, motherhood, and relationship to Christ.

Ignatius of Antioch, writing in the early second century, emphasized that Jesus was truly born of a virgin.

Justin Martyr and Irenaeus developed the comparison between Eve and Mary.

The Catholic Catechism preserves several of these early testimonies and specifically cites Irenaeus concerning Mary and Eve.

Catechism — Early Christian Witnesses to Mary

Was Theotokos Invented in A.D. 431?

The Council of Ephesus in A.D. 431 was extremely important in formally defending the title Theotokos, but the theological concept did not simply originate at the Council itself.

The Council's documents demonstrate that the title was being discussed in connection with the Church's understanding of Christ's identity.

Mary in Orthodox and Protestant Tradition

Discussions of Marian doctrine sometimes proceed as though there are only two positions — Catholic affirmation and Protestant rejection. The reality is more layered, and Eastern Orthodox Christianity in particular occupies a distinct position that predates the Catholic-Protestant division entirely.

Eastern Orthodoxy

Orthodox Christianity fully affirms Theotokos — indeed, the Council of Ephesus took place in an undivided Church, and the title is treasured equally in Orthodox liturgy and hymnography. Orthodox tradition also affirms Mary's perpetual virginity and venerates her intercession extensively.

However, Orthodox theology does not accept the Catholic dogma of the Immaculate Conception as formally defined in 1854, largely because Orthodox theology does not operate with the Western Augustinian framework of inherited guilt in the same way. Orthodoxy instead commemorates Mary's falling asleep as the Dormition of the Theotokos rather than using the specific Western term "Assumption," though the underlying belief that Mary was taken into heavenly glory is shared by both traditions.

Protestantism

Protestant tradition is far from uniform on this question. The magisterial Reformers themselves are frequently misunderstood here: Martin Luther continued to affirm the title "Mother of God" throughout his life, and John Calvin likewise accepted Theotokos as a legitimate and important Christological safeguard, even while both rejected Marian intercession, the Immaculate Conception, and the Assumption as unbiblical accretions.

Most contemporary Protestant traditions honor Mary's obedience and role in the incarnation as a biblical example of faith, while rejecting any doctrine or devotional practice not explicitly grounded in Scripture — which is why prayers to Mary, the Rosary, and the later Catholic dogmas are generally not practiced in Protestant churches.

Mary's "Yes" to God

Mary's response to the angel is one of the most important statements concerning her character:

"Behold, I am the handmaid of the Lord; let it be to me according to your word." — Luke 1:38

Her response has traditionally been understood as an act of faith and obedience.

This is one reason the early Christian tradition developed the comparison between Mary and Eve.

"All Generations Will Call Me Blessed"

"For behold, henceforth all generations will call me blessed." — Luke 1:48

But Mary immediately explains why:

"For he who is mighty has done great things for me." — Luke 1:49

Mary's biblical language is therefore fundamentally God-centered. Her greatness is presented as the result of what God has done.

"My soul magnifies the Lord." — Luke 1:46

What Catholics Do Not Mean by "Mother of God"

Mary is a goddess.
Mary created God's divine nature.
Mary existed before God.
Mary is equal to the Trinity.
Mary is a fourth person of the Trinity.
Mary replaces Jesus Christ.
Mary independently saves humanity.
Mary possesses divine nature.

Instead, Catholic theology understands Mary's privileges as gifts of God's grace and as completely dependent upon Christ.

Mary and the Development of Christian Doctrine

One of the major differences between Catholic and many Protestant approaches to Marian doctrine concerns the question of doctrinal authority and development.

Catholic theology understands Sacred Scripture within the wider context of apostolic Tradition and the teaching authority of the Church.

Consequently, Catholic theology does not require every dogma to appear in Scripture as one explicit sentence.

This becomes particularly important when discussing the Immaculate Conception and the Assumption.

Critics may argue that these doctrines are later developments because Scripture does not explicitly narrate them. Catholic theology responds that doctrines may develop in their explicit formulation while remaining, in Catholic understanding, faithful to the apostolic deposit of faith.

Modern Biblical Scholarship

Modern scholarship does not have one single interpretation of every Marian passage. Catholic, Protestant, Orthodox, Anglican, and non-confessional scholars have proposed different interpretations of the biblical and historical evidence.

The Catholic Position in Summary

Mary is the Virgin Mother of Jesus Christ.
Jesus Christ is the eternal Son of God who became truly man.
Mary therefore truly gave birth to the divine Person of the Son according to his humanity.
For this reason the Catholic Church calls Mary Theotokos — Mother of God.
Her other Marian privileges are understood in relation to Christ and the mystery of the Incarnation.

Conclusion

The Catholic teaching that Mary is the Mother of God should not be understood as the claim that Mary is divine or that she created God.

Rather, it is a confession about the identity of Jesus Christ.

The eternal Word truly became flesh. That Word was born from Mary. The one Person whom Mary bore is the eternal Son of God.

Therefore the title Theotokos expresses the Christian confession that Jesus Christ is truly God and truly man.

The New Testament presents Mary as the virgin who conceives Christ through the Holy Spirit, the mother of the Lord, the obedient handmaid of God, the woman who directs attention toward Jesus at Cana, the mother standing beneath the Cross, and the disciple praying with the early Church.

Catholic theology subsequently developed a wider Marian doctrine concerning her perpetual virginity, Immaculate Conception, Assumption, spiritual motherhood, intercession, and relationship to the Church — while Orthodox and Protestant traditions each engage this same biblical and historical material and arrive at meaningfully different conclusions, as the comparison above shows.

Not all of these doctrines are stated with the same degree of explicitness in Scripture. Some are direct biblical affirmations, while others are theological conclusions developed through Scripture, apostolic Tradition, the testimony of the Fathers, and the Church's doctrinal definitions.

Ultimately, the Marian question brings us back to the central Christian question:

Who is Jesus Christ?

If Jesus Christ is truly the eternal Word made flesh, then the title Theotokos — Mother of God is fundamentally a statement about Christ himself.

This is why the title became so important in the controversy that culminated in the Council of Ephesus in A.D. 431 — and why, across a millennium and a half of division, Catholic and Orthodox Christians still share this one confession in common.
