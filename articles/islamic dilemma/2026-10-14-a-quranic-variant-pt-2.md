## Introduction

An earlier article in this series looked at Quran 37:12, where two accepted readings disagree about who is astonished. This article looks at a different kind of variant. Quran 36:38 speaks of the sun's course, the Prophet's explanation of that verse in the reports of Abu Dharr describes where the sun goes, and one reading attributed to two early Companions changes the verse's meaning. Taken together these sources raise questions about the text, about early reports of the Companions' codices, and about how the tradition understands the sun's motion.

The aim is to set out what the sources say, to include the answers that Muslim scholars give, and to separate what the evidence establishes from what is only claimed.

## The Verse

The standard text reads *wa-sh-shamsu tajrī li-mustaqarrin lahā dhālika taqdīru l-ʿazīzi l-ʿalīm*. Hilali and Khan translate it: "And the sun runs on its fixed course for a term (appointed). That is the Decree of the All-Mighty, the All-Knowing." The key word is *mustaqarr*, which classical commentators take either as a place of rest or as an appointed term. Ibn Kathir records both views in his commentary on this verse. The first is that the sun has a fixed place beneath the Throne, and the second is that it has an appointed time that ends on the Day of Resurrection.

## What the Hadith Reports Say

The first view rests on reports from Abu Dharr. In Sahih al-Bukhari (3199) the Prophet asks him at sunset, "Do you know where the sun goes?" and then says that it goes until it prostrates beneath the Throne, asks permission to rise, and is permitted, until a time comes when it will be told to return from where it came and will rise in the west. He connects this with Quran 36:38 ([Bukhari 3199](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:3199)). Other reports have Abu Dharr asking about the verse and hearing, "Its fixed course is underneath Allah's Throne" (Bukhari 4803 and 7433). The longer version in Sahih Muslim (159) describes the sun reaching its resting place beneath the Throne, prostrating, and being told each time to return, until the day when it is told to rise from where it sets.

A different wording is in Sunan Abu Dawud (4002), where Abu Dharr reports that the Prophet said the sun "sets in a spring of water" (*fī ʿaynin ḥāmiyah*), and Al-Albani graded the chain authentic ([Abu Dawud 4002](https://sunnah.com/abudawud:4002)). This language recalls Quran 18:86, where Dhul-Qarnayn reaches the place where the sun sets and finds it setting in a spring.

One detail is easy to miss. In Bukhari 7424 and in the wording of Muslim 159, the Prophet recited Quran 36:38 "as it is recited by Abdullah," that is, Abdullah ibn Masud. In the version of Muslim quoted in the English collections, the recitation ends "and that is a resting place for it." The reports in the two most respected collections therefore attribute to Ibn Masud a recitation that affirms a resting place. This will matter in what follows.

## The Reading Attributed to Ibn Masud and Ibn Abbas

Ibn Kathir adds that Ibn Masud and Ibn Abbas recited the verse differently: *wa-sh-shamsu tajrī lā mustaqarra lahā*, "and the sun runs with no fixed course (or resting place)." He explains the sense as meaning that the sun has no destination and does not settle in one place but keeps moving night and day, as in Quran 14:33, which describes the sun and moon as "constantly pursuing their courses" ([Ibn Kathir on 36:38](https://www.alim.org/quran/tafsir/ibn-kathir/surah/36/37)).

This reading differs from the standard text in its consonants. The standard *li-mustaqarrin* is written with a *lām* attached to the noun, while the reported reading has an additional *alif* to make the separate negation *lā*. It is therefore a difference of text, not of vowels, and so it belongs to the class of reports about the Companions' personal codices, which scholars have collected (see Arthur Jeffery, *Materials for the History of the Text of the Qur'an*, 1937). Muslim scholars classify such readings as *shādhdh*, irregular, and not among the ten accepted readings recited today.

## Who Were Ibn Masud and Ibn Abbas?

Both men are well known in the sources. Abdullah ibn Amr reports that the Prophet told people to learn the Quran from four men, and he named Ibn Masud first ([Sahih Muslim, book 44, 164](https://sunnah.com/muslim/44/164); the same report is in Sahih al-Bukhari, [3808](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:3808)). Ibn Abbas, the Prophet's cousin, was embraced by him with a prayer that God would give him knowledge of the Book and wisdom ([Bukhari 75](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:75); [Bukhari 3756](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:3756)). Reports about their readings therefore carry weight with Muslims and with historians of the text.

## The Spring in Quran 18:86

The "spring" language also has a reading history. Quran 18:86 says that Dhul-Qarnayn found the sun "setting in a spring" described either as *ḥamiʾa* (murky) or *ḥāmiya* (hot). Ibn Amir, Hamza, al-Kisai and Abu Bakr (Shuba) read *ḥāmiya*, and the others read *ḥamiʾa* ([Quran.com, readings of 18:86](https://quran.com/18/86/qiraat); [Abu Dawud 3986](https://sunnah.com/abudawud:3986)). A report in al-Tirmidhi (2934) says that Ibn Abbas and Amr ibn al-As disagreed about how to read the word and took the matter to Kab al-Ahbar, and al-Tirmidhi remarks that if either had a transmission from the Prophet he would not have needed to ask ([Tirmidhi 2934](https://sunnah.com/tirmidhi:2934)).

Quran.com's note on these readings says that they are complementary descriptions of the water, "from the perspective of Dhul-Qarnayn," following the commentator al-Mahdawi. That points to the Muslim answer discussed next.

## How Muslim Scholars Respond

Several responses are standard.

- **The verse describes appearance.** Quran 18:86 says that Dhul-Qarnayn "found" (*wajada*) the sun setting in a spring. Many commentators take this as the way it looked to him, as an observer at the edge of the sea would see it, and not as a statement about astronomy.
- **The prostration is submission.** Quran 22:18 says that the sun, moon and stars bow down to God. Commentators often take the hadith of the prostration as describing this cosmic submission, in a way known to God, and not as a description of the sun's physical movement.
- **The sun is always beneath the Throne.** Because the Throne encompasses creation, some scholars explain that wherever the sun is, it is beneath the Throne, so "going beneath the Throne" does not require a journey to a place.
- **The variant is not part of the recited Quran.** The reading *lā mustaqarra lahā* is irregular and is not recited in prayer. Muslim scholars typically treat such reports as evidence of early personal copies or explanatory glosses, which the standardisation of the text under Uthman set aside.
- **The attributions are mixed.** As noted above, the Sahih reports have the Prophet reciting the verse "as Abdullah recites it," in a form that affirms a resting place. That is difficult to reconcile with the reading attributed to Ibn Masud in Ibn Kathir's commentary, and some scholars would conclude that the attribution to him is uncertain.

## What the Evidence Shows

Several points can be stated with confidence. The reading *lā mustaqarra lahā* is reported by Ibn Kathir from Ibn Masud and Ibn Abbas, and it differs from the standard text in its consonants. Muslim scholars know of it and class it as irregular. The reports about these two Companions' readings are not uniform, since the Sahih reports describe Ibn Masud's recitation in a way that conflicts with the report quoted by Ibn Kathir. And Ibn Kathir himself understood the Throne as a dome and not a sphere, which shows how the classical commentators pictured the cosmos.

Other claims go beyond the evidence. The existence of one irregular reading does not by itself show that the text in use today is wrong, and the inconsistent attributions make it hard to say with certainty what Ibn Masud actually recited. The question that remains is a historical and theological one: how does the tradition know which of the early reports of the Companions' readings is reliable, and on what basis was the standard text fixed? Muslim scholars answer that question through the principle of continuous transmission (*tawātur*) and the agreement of the community on the Uthmanic text.

## Conclusion

Quran 36:38 shows how a single verse can connect the text, the hadith and early reports about the Companions' readings. The standard reading speaks of the sun's resting place or appointed term, the hadith of Abu Dharr places that resting place beneath the Throne, and an irregular reading attributed to Ibn Masud and Ibn Abbas denies a fixed course. The sources show that the variant is real but not canonical, that the reports about it are not uniform, and that Muslim scholars have a range of responses. A responsible assessment keeps those facts in view and does not claim more than the evidence supports.
