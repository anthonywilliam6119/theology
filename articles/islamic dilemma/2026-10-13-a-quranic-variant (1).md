## Introduction

The Qur'an is recited through recognized reading traditions (*qira'at*), and discussions of these readings raise important questions about how the text has been transmitted and interpreted. Muslim scholars generally distinguish between legitimate canonical readings and claims that the Qur'an has been corrupted. The existence of variant readings, by itself, does not settle that debate; each reading must be examined in its linguistic, historical, and theological context.

This article considers one example often discussed in debates about Qur'anic readings: Qur'an 37:12. The familiar reading addresses Muhammad as the one who is astonished, while another reading reported in early exegetical and textual discussions uses a first-person form that can be rendered, "Rather, I am amazed, while they mock." The difference matters because it changes who is presented as experiencing astonishment.

## Two Ways of Reading Qur'an 37:12

In the translation known as *The Clear Quran*, the verse is rendered:

> “In fact, you are astonished by their denial, while they ridicule you.” — Qur'an 37:12 ([*The Clear Quran*](https://www.islamawakened.com/quran/37/st69.htm))

Readers can compare the translation and verse at the link above.

An alternative form is commonly transliterated as *'ajibtu* rather than *'ajibta*. In English, this shifts the statement from "you are amazed" to "I am amazed." In the latter wording, the speaker is understood to be God, who is described as being amazed at the mockery of the rejecters. The precise wording and grammatical explanation depend on the reading tradition and the source being consulted, so the two forms should not be treated as interchangeable without explanation.

## Ibn Taymiyyah and Divine Attributes

The medieval scholar Ibn Taymiyyah discussed divine attributes such as amazement, laughter, and looking in his theological writings. In the material quoted and explained by Muhammad ibn Salih al-'Uthaymin in his commentary on *al-'Aqidah al-Wasitiyyah*, Qur'an 37:12 is cited in support of affirming amazement as an attribute of God. The commentary distinguishes between amazement caused by a lack of knowledge, which it rejects as impossible for God, and amazement understood as a response to something extraordinary or inappropriate.

On this interpretation, the wording does not mean that God discovers information previously unknown to Him. Rather, the authors argue that the attribute can be affirmed without implying ignorance or weakness. This is a theological interpretation, not a conclusion that every Muslim school necessarily expresses in the same way. Other Muslim theologians have approached descriptions of divine attributes differently, including through figurative interpretation or by declining to specify how such attributes apply to God.

## Why the Variant Is Discussed

The alternate reading has attracted attention because it changes the grammatical subject of the verse and is used in some theological arguments about divine attributes. Fadel Soliman's *Bridges' Translation of the Ten Qira'at of the Noble Qur'an* also notes variant renderings for this verse. Readers wishing to investigate the issue should consult the translation's discussion directly and compare it with specialist studies of the *qira'at*, rather than relying only on polemical summaries.

One frequently repeated claim in interfaith debate is that any variant reading necessarily proves textual corruption or a doctrinal contradiction. That conclusion does not follow automatically. A variant may be part of a recognized reading tradition, and the question of how it relates to the received text requires historical and textual analysis. At the same time, it is fair to ask whether a particular variant changes the emphasis or meaning of a passage, and whether theologians have interpreted it differently.

## The Role of Early Authorities

Abdullah ibn Mas'ud is important in this discussion because Islamic tradition remembers him as one of the Companions from whom Muslims were encouraged to learn Qur'anic recitation. Reports naming him among these teachers appear in both *Sahih al-Bukhari* and *Sahih Muslim*. These reports establish his significance in the history of Qur'anic learning; they do not, on their own, establish every claim made about the precise wording of a particular verse in a manuscript associated with him.

Readers can consult the relevant reports here: [Sahih al-Bukhari](https://www.searchtruth.com/book_display.php?book=58&translator=1&start=0&number=153); [Sahih Muslim](https://sunnah.com/muslim/44/164); and [another version](https://www.searchtruth.com/book_display.php?book=031&translator=2&start=0&number=6008). The reports should be read in their full context and alongside specialist scholarship on early Qur'anic manuscripts and reading traditions.

## Conclusion

Qur'an 37:12 provides a useful case study in how a small grammatical difference can influence interpretation. One familiar reading presents Muhammad as astonished at the disbelievers' response; an alternative reading reported in the tradition presents the speaker as saying, "I am amazed." Ibn Taymiyyah and al-'Uthaymin draw on the latter form in their discussion of divine attributes, while other theologians may explain such language differently.

The responsible conclusion is neither to dismiss the variant without examination nor to claim that its existence alone settles the entire question of Qur'anic preservation. A careful assessment should distinguish the history of the readings, the reliability and status attributed to each reading, and the theological conclusions scholars draw from them. These are related questions, but they are not identical.
