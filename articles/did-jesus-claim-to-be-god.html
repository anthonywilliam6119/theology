Did Jesus Claim to Be God?

By Anthony William · Published September 24, 2026

Few questions have generated as much theological and historical discussion as the question: Did Jesus of Nazareth claim to be God?

For Christians, the answer is fundamentally yes. Christianity teaches that Jesus Christ is not merely a prophet, teacher, Messiah or messenger of God, but God the Son who became human.

Judaism rejects this conclusion. Jewish theology maintains that God is one and that Jesus cannot be identified with God Himself. Rabbinic interpreters therefore read the relevant biblical passages differently from Christian theologians.

Modern biblical scholarship adds another important distinction. Scholars often differentiate between what can be established about the historical Jesus, what the Gospel writers claim about Jesus, and what later Christian theology formulated concerning the nature of Christ.

Consequently, the question should not be reduced to: "Did Jesus ever say the exact words, 'I am God'?"

The more historically meaningful questions are:

  • What did Jesus say about himself?
  • What authority did he claim?
  • How did his Jewish contemporaries understand those claims?
  • How did the earliest Christians interpret him?
  • And how did the Church Fathers eventually formulate the doctrine of Christ's divinity?

This article examines these questions from Jewish, Christian and historical perspectives.

The first distinction: Jesus did not say the exact words "I am God"

One point should be established at the beginning. The canonical Gospels do not record Jesus saying the precise sentence: "I am God."

This is important because sometimes Christian apologetic arguments are presented as though Jesus explicitly made that statement. He did not use those exact words in the Gospel narratives.

However, Christianity has never depended exclusively upon such a sentence. The Christian argument is that Jesus made claims concerning his pre-existence, authority, relationship with God, ability to forgive sins, authority over the Sabbath, role in judgment, and relationship with the Father that together amount to a claim far beyond that of an ordinary prophet.

This is particularly evident in the Gospel of John.

The Gospel of John and the divine identity of Jesus

John begins his Gospel with extraordinarily high language concerning Jesus:

"In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." — John 1:1

A few verses later John says:

"And the Word became flesh." — John 1:14

The Christian interpretation is straightforward: The Word is divine, and the Word became human in Jesus Christ. This means that, according to John, Jesus' existence did not begin with his birth in Bethlehem. His identity reaches back to "the beginning."

This is one of the most important differences between the Christian understanding of Jesus and the ordinary Jewish understanding of a prophet. A prophet is a human being who receives God's message. John's Jesus is presented as the pre-existent Word through whom creation itself came into existence.

"Before Abraham was, I am"

One of the most debated statements of Jesus appears in John 8:58:

"Before Abraham was, I am." — John 8:58

The statement is unusual. Jesus does not merely say: "Before Abraham was, I was." Instead, John gives him the expression: ἐγώ εἰμι — egō eimi literally: "I am."

Christian interpreters have traditionally connected this statement with Exodus 3:14, where God reveals Himself to Moses: "I AM WHO I AM." This has led Christian theologians to understand John 8:58 as a claim to divine pre-existence and, potentially, an allusion to the divine name.

However, scholarly caution is necessary. The Greek expression egō eimi does not automatically mean "I am God" every time it appears. The expression can function in ordinary Greek as "I am he" or "it is I." Therefore, the theological meaning has to be established from the context of John's Gospel as a whole.

"I and the Father are one"

John 10:30 contains another famous statement:

"I and the Father are one." — John 10:30

But the reaction of Jesus' opponents is equally important:

"You, being a man, make yourself God." — John 10:33

However, Jesus does not simply respond: "Yes, exactly. I am God." Instead, he quotes Psalm 82: "I said, you are gods." (John 10:34). This makes the passage more complicated than it sometimes appears in apologetic discussions. Jewish interpreters can point to the Psalm 82 quotation and argue that the language of "gods" or divine representation can sometimes be applied to human beings acting with authority from God.

"My Lord and my God"

One of the strongest passages in the entire New Testament occurs after the resurrection. Thomas sees the risen Jesus and says:

"My Lord and my God!" — John 20:28

This is particularly important because the statement is addressed directly to Jesus. John does not correct Thomas. Instead, Jesus responds by discussing faith. Christian interpreters therefore understand this as an explicit confession of Jesus' divine identity.

Jesus and the forgiveness of sins

In Mark 2, Jesus tells a paralyzed man: "Son, your sins are forgiven." (Mark 2:5). The scribes immediately object: "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (Mark 2:7).

The passage is important because Jesus does not merely tell the man: "God has forgiven you." He personally declares: "Your sins are forgiven." Christian interpretation understands this as evidence that Jesus possesses an authority belonging to God. A Jewish or non-Trinitarian interpretation can respond that God may delegate authority to a human representative.

Jesus as judge of humanity

The New Testament also presents Jesus as the future judge. In John 5:22, Jesus says:

"The Father judges no one, but has entrusted all judgment to the Son." — John 5:22

This is a remarkable statement. In Jewish thought, God is ultimately the Judge. The Christian interpretation is that Jesus participates in God's eschatological authority.

Jesus' relationship with the Father

There is, however, another side of the evidence. Jesus frequently distinguishes himself from the Father. He prays to God. He says that the Father sent him.

He says: "The Father is greater than I." (John 14:28). In Mark 13:32, Jesus says concerning the day and hour: "But concerning that day or that hour, no one knows... neither the Son, but only the Father."

Classical Christianity developed a sophisticated answer. Jesus is understood as fully God and fully human. According to this doctrine, some statements concern his humanity, while others concern his divine identity.

Jewish monotheism and the identity of God

To understand the Jewish objection, we must understand the Jewish concept of God. The central confession of Judaism is the Shema:

"Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one." — Deuteronomy 6:4

Jewish theology takes God's uniqueness and oneness with enormous seriousness. Therefore, when Christians say: "Jesus is God, but the Father is God, and the Holy Spirit is God, yet there is only one God," Judaism does not recognize this as a legitimate development of biblical monotheism.

Deuteronomy 18:15 — "A prophet like Moses"

Christians frequently connect this prophecy with Jesus because Peter explicitly applies the passage to Jesus in Acts 3:22–23. But Jewish interpretation is substantially different.

The famous Jewish commentator Rashi explains: "One who is as I am, from your midst, of your brethren." He understands the promise in terms of prophet succeeding prophet. Ibn Ezra explains "like me" as meaning a prophet of God rather than a diviner. Rashbam similarly emphasizes that the prophet will teach Israel God's commandments. Modern scholar Robert Alter explains that the future prophet is "like Moses" particularly in function — acting as an intermediary between God and Israel.

Traditional Jewish commentators do not identify the prophet in Deuteronomy 18 with Jesus. This means that the disagreement between Christianity and Judaism begins at the level of interpretive framework.

Paul and the divinity of Christ

Paul's letters are earlier than the written Gospels and are therefore extremely important for historical study. In Philippians 2:6–11, Christ is described as existing in the form of God, becoming human, dying, and subsequently being exalted so that every knee bows to him.

Another important passage is 1 Corinthians 8:6: "Yet for us there is one God, the Father... and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things." Scholars have noted the importance of this passage because Paul appears to include Jesus within a reformulation of Jewish monotheistic confession.

The earliest Church Fathers

Ignatius of Antioch (early second century) refers to Jesus Christ as: "our God." His writings demonstrate that explicit belief in Christ's divinity was already present among Christian communities close to the apostolic generation.

Justin Martyr (second century) argues in his Dialogue with Trypho that Christ existed before his human birth and identifies Christ with the divine Word.

Irenaeus of Lyons speaks of Christ as: "our Lord, and God, and Saviour, and King." For Irenaeus, Jesus' divinity is connected with the Christian doctrine of salvation.

Tertullian (beginning of the third century) distinguished the Father, Son and Holy Spirit while maintaining that Christianity believes in one God, marking an important milestone in Trinitarian terminology.

Did the Council of Nicaea invent the divinity of Jesus?

A common claim is: "Jesus was not considered God until the Council of Nicaea in 325." Historically, this is difficult to sustain. The writings of Ignatius, Justin Martyr and Irenaeus predate Nicaea and contain explicit claims about Jesus' divine status.

Rather, Nicaea addressed a major controversy concerning how the Son relates to the Father. The Nicene Creed describes Jesus as: "God of God, Light of Light, very God of very God, begotten, not made, being of one substance with the Father." The Council was not introducing an entirely new belief, but defining the boundaries of orthodox Christian belief about Christ.

A comparison of Jewish and Christian interpretation

Christian Interpretation

On Jesus' Identity Jesus is the pre-existent divine Word (Logos) who became flesh. His claims to pre-existence, forgiving sins, and receiving worship point to his intrinsic divine nature.

Jewish Interpretation

On Jesus' Identity God is absolutely one (Shema). Jesus is viewed as a human teacher or prophet, but not divine. Attributing divinity to a human violates strict monotheism.

Christian Interpretation

On "Son of God" A unique metaphysical title indicating Jesus shares the same divine essence as the Father, eternally begotten, not created.

Jewish Interpretation

On "Son of God" A metaphorical title used in the Hebrew Bible for the nation of Israel, the Davidic king, or righteous individuals, not indicating literal divinity.

Christian Interpretation

On Deuteronomy 18:15 Fulfilled in Jesus as the ultimate Prophet like Moses, who not only speaks God's words but is God incarnate, as affirmed in Acts 3:22-23.

Jewish Interpretation

On Deuteronomy 18:15 Refers to the succession of human prophets within Israel (as explained by Rashi and Ibn Ezra), not a prediction of God becoming human.

Final conclusion

So, did Jesus claim to be God? The most careful answer is: Jesus is not recorded as saying the exact words "I am God."

However, the New Testament — especially the Gospel of John — presents Jesus as making claims that Christians understand as claims to divine identity. He speaks of pre-existence before Abraham. He claims unique unity with the Father. He exercises authority to forgive sins. And Thomas addresses the risen Jesus: "My Lord and my God."

The Jewish interpretation reaches a different conclusion. Rabbinic commentators interpret prophetic passages within the framework of Israelite prophecy and do not understand them as predictions that God would become a human being.

The early Church Fathers, on the other hand, demonstrate that Christians were calling Jesus divine well before the Council of Nicaea. Nicaea therefore did not suddenly invent the divinity of Christ in the fourth century. Rather, it gave a formal theological definition to a dispute that had been developing within Christianity for generations.

What can be established historically is that belief in Jesus' divine status arose extremely early in Christianity and was not first invented at Nicaea. What Christians and Jews disagree about is what that early testimony actually means about the identity of God and Jesus.
